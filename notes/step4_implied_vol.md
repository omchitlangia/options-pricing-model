Part 1 — Motivation for Implied Volatility
In Step 3, using historical volatility in the Black–Scholes model led to systematic mispricing when compared with market option prices. The errors varied with moneyness and time to maturity, showing that the constant volatility assumption fails. This issue points to volatility because all other Black–Scholes inputs are directly observed from the market, while volatility is externally assumed. Hence, persistent pricing errors indicate that the volatility assumption is incorrect. Implied volatility is the natural next step because it allows volatility to be treated as a market-implied quantity rather than a fixed assumption, directly addressing the failure of the constant-volatility assumption observed in Step 3.

Part 2 — What Implied Volatility Represents
Implied volatility is the volatility level implied by the market price of an option within the Black–Scholes framework, representing the market-consistent volatility for that specific strike and maturity. It differs from historical volatility because historical volatility is computed from past returns of the underlying asset, whereas implied volatility is derived from current option prices and varies across options. Implied volatility is not a forecast or prediction because it reflects how volatility is currently priced by the market, not what volatility will actually be realized in the future.

Part 3 — Inverting the Black–Scholes Model
The Black–Scholes formula cannot be inverted analytically for volatility because volatility appears inside the cumulative normal distribution terms, making it impossible to isolate volatility in closed form. As a result, pricing an option for a given market price becomes a root-finding problem. Solving for volatility numerically means using iterative numerical methods to find the volatility value that makes the Black–Scholes price equal to the observed market price, gradually converging to the correct solution. The solution is unique because the option price is a continuous and strictly increasing function of volatility. Since Vega is always positive, a single option price corresponds to only one implied volatility.

Part 4 — Numerical Method Used
A numerical root-finding approach was required because the Black–Scholes equation cannot be solved analytically for volatility, making implied volatility estimation a root-finding problem. The bisection method was chosen because it is robust and does not rely on derivatives. By repeatedly halving a bounded interval, it guarantees convergence, unlike Newton–Raphson which, although faster, can fail to converge if the initial guess is poor or Vega is unstable. Convergence is guaranteed in this context because the option price is a continuous and strictly increasing function of volatility, ensuring a single root within the chosen volatility bounds.

Part 5 — Dataset-Level Implied Volatility Extraction
To compute implied volatility for each option, the Black–Scholes pricing equation was framed as a root-finding problem. A lower bound for volatility was set close to zero (1e-6) and an upper bound was set high (3.0). At each iteration, the option price was computed at the midpoint volatility. If the calculated price was higher than the market price, the upper bound was reduced; otherwise, the lower bound was increased. This process was repeated until the difference between the Black–Scholes price and the market price approached zero. The inputs taken from the market were the option’s market price, underlying spot price, strike price, time to expiry, and the risk-free rate. It is meaningful to compute implied volatility option-by-option because implied volatility is not constant. It varies systematically across options depending on moneyness and time to maturity, which cannot be captured using a single volatility estimate.

Part 6 — Distribution of Implied Volatility
The overall distribution of implied volatility was right-skewed. Most options were concentrated in the lower volatility range, with the median implied volatility at around 0.129 and the interquartile range roughly between 0.109 and 0.173. However, a long right tail was present, with implied volatility extending up to approximately 0.399. The mean implied volatility (≈ 0.151) was higher than the historical volatility used earlier, indicating that option prices embed higher risk than what is observed in past realized returns. The right-skewed distribution implies that while the majority of options reflect moderate expected volatility, a smaller set of options carries significantly higher implied volatility, suggesting strong market demand for protection against tail risks and extreme market moves.

Part 7 — Implied Volatility vs Moneyness
Implied volatility clearly changes with moneyness. In the data, ATM call options have the lowest average implied volatility (around 0.125). OTM calls show slightly higher implied volatility (around 0.146), while ITM calls have much higher implied volatility (around 0.252). This shows that volatility is not constant across strikes and increases as options move away from ATM. ATM implied volatility acts as a benchmark because ATM options are the most actively traded and most sensitive to volatility, so their prices best reflect the market’s base volatility expectation. This pattern explains the mispricing seen in Step 3. Using one historical volatility worked relatively better for ATM options but failed for ITM and OTM options, creating systematic pricing errors across moneyness.

Part 8 — Implied Volatility vs Maturity
Implied volatility decreases with time to maturity in our dataset. Short-dated options have a higher average implied volatility (around 0.182), while medium-dated options show lower implied volatility (around 0.137). This indicates a downward-sloping implied volatility term structure. A downward-sloping term structure suggests that the market expects higher uncertainty or risk in the near term, often due to upcoming events or short-term uncertainty. An upward-sloping term structure, in contrast, would imply greater uncertainty further into the future. This behavior violates the constant-volatility assumption of Black–Scholes because volatility clearly depends on time to maturity. Using a single volatility value across all maturities cannot capture this variation, leading to systematic mispricing across short- and longer-dated options

Part 9 — Key Findings from Step 4
The main empirical conclusion of Step 4 is that implied volatility is not constant and varies systematically across moneyness and time to maturity. Once implied volatility is extracted option-by-option, the structured pricing errors observed earlier under Black–Scholes largely disappear. Implied volatility resolves the earlier mispricing because it replaces the incorrect constant volatility assumption with market-implied volatility levels that differ across options. By allowing volatility to vary, Black–Scholes prices align much more closely with observed market prices. This reveals that markets do not price risk using a single volatility estimate. Instead, risk is priced differently across strikes and maturities, reflecting asymmetric risk perceptions, demand for tail protection, and time-dependent uncertainty.

Part 10 — Limitations and Scope
Even after introducing implied volatility, several Black–Scholes assumptions are still retained. The model continues to assume log-normal price dynamics, frictionless markets, continuous trading, and risk-neutral pricing. Implied volatility does not fully “fix” Black–Scholes because it is not a model of volatility itself. It is an implied parameter backed out from prices, not a mechanism that explains how volatility evolves over time or across states of the market. Important market features remain missing, such as stochastic volatility, volatility clustering, jumps in prices, and liquidity effects. As a result, implied volatility improves pricing consistency but does not provide a complete structural description of market risk.
