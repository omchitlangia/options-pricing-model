Part 1 — Why Greeks are needed after pricing
Pricing alone is not enough to understand the model performance as it won't tell us how the options price is changing with respect to the changes in the underlying variable. Two models can produce similar prices but behave very differently when the underlying price, volatility, or time changes, which pricing errors alone cannot reveal. After implied volatility analysis, the key unanswered question is how sensitive option prices are to small changes in market inputs. While implied volatility explains cross-sectional pricing differences, it does not show where the model is fragile or how risk is distributed across price, volatility, and time. Greeks shift the focus from valuation to risk sensitivity by quantifying how option prices change with respect to underlying drivers such as spot price, volatility, and time. This allows us to analyze not just whether a model prices options correctly, but how it manages and distributes risk across different market conditions.

Part 2 — What Greeks represent conceptually
A Greek measures how sensitive an option’s price is to a small change in one of its underlying inputs, such as the asset price, volatility, or time. In general terms, Greeks describe how the option value reacts locally to changes in market conditions rather than the value of the option itself. Greeks are local, or infinitesimal, quantities because they are defined as derivatives evaluated at the current market state. They describe the immediate rate of change around a specific point, not behavior over large moves or long horizons. Greeks are not predictions because they do not describe what will happen in the market. They only describe how the option price would respond if a small change occurs, assuming all other model inputs remain unchanged. Part 3 — Delta: Sensitivity to the underlying
Delta measures how much the price of a call option changes for a small change in the underlying asset price. It represents the option’s first-order sensitivity to movements in the underlying. For call options, Delta varies systematically with moneyness. OTM calls have Delta close to 0, ATM calls have Delta around 0.5, and ITM calls have Delta close to 1. This reflects how likely the option is to finish in the money as the underlying price changes. Delta approaches 0 for OTM calls because small changes in the underlying have little effect on the option’s payoff when it is far from the strike. At-the-money, the option has roughly equal chances of expiring in or out of the money, so Delta is around 0.5. Deep ITM calls behave similarly to the underlying itself, so their Delta approaches 1.

Part 4 — Gamma: Instability of Delta
Gamma measures how quickly Delta changes with respect to changes in the underlying price. It captures the curvature of the option price and indicates how stable or unstable Delta is. Gamma is highest near at-the-money options because this is the region where small changes in the underlying price most strongly affect whether the option finishes in or out of the money. As a result, Delta transitions rapidly around the ATM region. High Gamma implies hedging instability because when Delta changes quickly, a hedge based on Delta becomes outdated almost immediately. This requires frequent rebalancing, increasing transaction costs and making risk management more difficult, especially during fast market moves.

Part 5 — Vega: Sensitivity to volatility
Vega measures how much an option’s price changes for a small change in implied volatility. It captures the option’s exposure to volatility rather than to price movements of the underlying. Vega peaks at at-the-money options because ATM options are most sensitive to changes in volatility. A change in volatility has the largest impact on the probability of the option finishing in the money when the option is near the strike. Vega generally increases with maturity because longer-dated options have more time for volatility to affect the underlying price. As a result, changes in implied volatility have a larger impact on option values for longer expiries. 

Part 6 — Theta: Time decay
Theta measures how fast an option’s value decays as time passes, holding all other factors constant. It represents the cost of holding optionality over time. Theta is typically negative for call options because, as time passes, the probability of the option finishing in the money decreases unless the underlying moves favorably. With less time remaining, the option loses time value. Theta is strongest for short-dated at-the-money options because this is where time value is most concentrated. Near expiration, ATM options lose value rapidly as the window for a meaningful price move shrinks, causing optionality to decay the fastest.
